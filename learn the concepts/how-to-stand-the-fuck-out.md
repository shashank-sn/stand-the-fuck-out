# How to Stand the F*ck Out

Four months into El Mejor Coffee, something was bothering me.

Sales were good. The product was genuinely beautiful. First-time buyers kept saying the coffee was incredible and the packaging was unlike anything they'd seen. By every surface-level metric, we were doing well.

But nobody came back.

I remember staring at the retention numbers one night, trying to figure out what I was missing. We had the product right. We had the customers. We had the momentum. And yet — zero repeat purchases. Something was broken and I couldn't see what.

Here's what I didn't understand at the time: good sales and strong positioning aren't the same thing.

You can sell something once by being interesting. Positioning is what makes people come back. And without it, every new customer you win is a customer you'll have to win again.

---

## The Sea of Sameness is Real

Walk into any category right now — coffee, SaaS, fitness, consulting — and you'll see the same five brands saying the same five things.

"Premium quality." "Trusted by thousands." "Customer-first approach." "Built for [insert audience]." "The all-in-one solution."

Nobody is lying. But nobody stands out either.

The trap isn't being bad. The trap is being average. Average looks safe, but it's actually the most expensive place to be — because you're spending money, time, and energy competing on dimensions where ten other people are already better funded.

My family has been in coffee for a long time. My uncle owns a coffee estate. So when I started El Mejor, I was carrying something Starbucks couldn't buy: a genuine, lived obsession with where coffee comes from and who grows it. I had an unfair advantage sitting right in front of me.

But I was so busy trying to be the "Apple of Coffees" — great design, premium feel, beautiful branding — that I almost missed what customers were actually raving about.

---

## Your Gut Alone Will Lie to You

Here's the thing about obsession: it's essential, but it's not enough.

When I started El Mejor, my gut said: build around farmer welfare and fresh roasting. Real coffee, roasted after you order, because the farmer deserves to know his work matters. That felt right. It felt true.

What I didn't do — not at first — was listen to what customers were actually responding to.

Then the emails started coming in.

"Thank you for putting my name on the box. I've never received coffee with my name on it before."

One customer. Then five. Then twenty.

Same message. Different words. Same pattern.

I had been so focused on my positioning thesis that I almost walked right past the real signal. The market wasn't telling me my thesis was wrong. It was telling me there was something *more* — people didn't just want great coffee, they wanted to feel seen. To feel like the brand actually knew they existed.

That's where El Mejor's real positioning locked in: not just farmer-first coffee, but coffee that carries the names of the farmers who grew it and the person who ordered it. A supply chain made personal at both ends.

The gut brought me to the door. The market showed me what was on the other side.

## What Positioning Actually Is

Most people think positioning is a marketing exercise — something you do with a tagline or a logo or a one-liner on your homepage.

It's not.

Positioning is the answer to a single question: why would someone choose you over everyone else?

Not "what do you do" — anyone can answer that. Not "what makes you better" — everyone says they're better. The real question is: what do you and only you credibly own?

For El Mejor, it was this: "We are the only coffee company that roasts and grinds after you order because we care more about farmer welfare than convenience."

That's the Onlyness Statement, a framework from Marty Neumeier. And when it works, it's almost uncomfortably specific. It names a category, a differentiator, and a reason — and all three have to be real. You can't fake it. Starbucks can't suddenly claim it. A competitor can't copy it overnight without lying.

The specificity is what makes it defensible.

"Better coffee" is forgettable. "Only roast after order" is a fact you can build a brand on.

## Every Brand Needs a Villain

Strong positioning isn't just about what you're for. It's about what you're against.

For El Mejor, the villain was mass-produced, faceless coffee that treats farmers like commodity inputs and customers like transaction numbers. It's a system that optimizes for scale and margin at the cost of the people who actually grow the product.

I attack that villain in everything. Not aggressively, not with finger-pointing at specific brands — but clearly. When you buy El Mejor, you know what you're walking away from.

For Happy Beginnings, a brand I worked on, the villain was the traditional wedding industry: overpriced, stuffy, performatively formal. The whole positioning was built around being the anti-wedding wedding brand. Meme marketing, irreverent tone, prices that didn't require a second mortgage. The villain made everything else sharp.

Name your villain. It doesn't have to be a competitor. It can be an approach, a norm, an industry habit. Something that's broken, that most people accept because they think it's just how things are.

Your villain gives your positioning a direction to fight in.

## The Anti-Positioning Move

One of the most counterintuitive things I've done in building brands is explicitly saying who I'm not for.

It feels scary. You're leaving customers on the table. You're narrowing your market.

You're actually doing the opposite.

When you tell the wrong customer "this isn't for you," the right customer leans in. Exclusion creates belonging. The people you *are* for suddenly feel like they found something made for them — not something trying to please everyone and ending up meaning nothing.

"We're not for people who just want cheap coffee" is a statement. It's a fence. And on the right side of that fence, your brand becomes a flag people want to stand under.

Brave positioning repels the wrong customers and magnetizes the right ones. You lose some potential buyers. You gain something more valuable: a real brand.

---

## How to Find Where You Stand

Start with what you can't fake.

Go through your last ten customer messages. Look for the one thing multiple people rave about without being prompted. Not general praise — specific praise. The exact sentence where someone describes why they chose you or why they came back.

That pattern is your signal. It's what the market is already telling you your positioning should be.

Then ask: what's true about you that took years to build? Your uncle's coffee estate. Your 15 years in the industry. Your obsession with a problem nobody else is willing to tackle. Your family's history. Your constraint that actually makes you sharper.

Put those two things together — what customers rave about and what you can't fake — and you're close to the intersection where real positioning lives.

It's not a tagline exercise. It's not a one-afternoon workshop. It's the most important strategic question your brand will ever answer.

And the brands that get it right don't just sell more. They build something people actually miss when it's gone.
