# Why Standing Out Is Actually a Business Strategy

I once watched a really good small agency destroy itself trying to be better.

Better service. Better communication. Better results. More testimonials. Bigger case studies. They kept improving, kept raising the bar on the same dimensions every other agency was competing on — and every quarter, they wondered why growth felt like pushing a boulder uphill.

Their product was genuinely excellent. Their clients loved them. And they were losing on price to agencies half as good because they couldn't explain why they cost more.

The problem wasn't quality. The problem was they were competing on a battlefield they'd never win.

---

## The Invisible Tax on Average Brands

There's a cost to being indistinguishable that most business owners never put a number on.

When you look like everyone else in your category, the only thing left to compete on is price. And when you compete on price, you're making yourself a commodity. You're training customers to think of you the same way they think of petrol — a thing they need, sure, but something they'll buy from whoever is cheapest that day.

The small agency had this problem. They were positioned as "personal attention and dedicated service" while the big agencies were positioned as "comprehensive teams and enterprise resources." Different words, same fight.

Both sides were losing.

Because nobody was claiming anything distinct. Both were fighting over the same idea of "who gives you more" — which is a race that ends in margin compression and burnout.

When I worked with them on repositioning, the breakthrough was realizing their size wasn't a weakness. It was the positioning.

Large agencies move slowly. Committees. Approvals. Layers between the brief and the execution. The small agency could do in 48 hours what a big agency needed two weeks of internal alignment to even start.

That's not "personal attention." That's agility. Speed. Moving at the speed of the market.

New name for what they did: Agile Marketing Partners.

Suddenly, the big agencies' "strength" — their teams, their resources, their established process — became the punchline. They weren't experienced. They were slow.

The result wasn't just a new tagline. It was a new sales conversation, a new pricing conversation, a new category they owned instead of one they were losing in.

## Positioning Is a Revenue Decision, Not a Marketing Decision

This is the part founders get wrong most often.

They treat positioning like a branding exercise — something the marketing team handles, something that lives in the "about us" page and the deck. It's the section of the business that feels important but not urgent.

But positioning is a filter that touches every single business decision.

Should you discount for Black Friday? Depends on your positioning. If you're "farmer-first coffee that roasts after you order," a 30% discount undercuts the story. Your pricing communicates your values. A bundle makes sense. A discount says "we don't really mean it."

Should you launch a new product? Depends on your positioning. If the new product doesn't reinforce your onlyness statement, it dilutes the thing you've spent years building. You lose before you launch.

Should you go on Amazon? Maybe. But if your positioning is built on a direct relationship between the customer and the origin of the product — if that intimacy is part of what you sell — then Amazon removes the very thing that makes you worth choosing.

Every decision runs through positioning, or it should. The brands that drift do so because they make each decision in isolation — what's profitable, what's trending, what the competitor is doing. Positioning gives you a north star that makes those decisions obvious.

## The Real Cost of Not Having One

When El Mejor was four months old, we had good sales and zero repeat customers.

That gap — between first purchase and second — is where most product businesses quietly bleed out. And the reason it happens is almost always the same: the customer bought the product, but they didn't buy into the brand. There was nothing to come back for except the product itself.

Once we sharpened the positioning — the farmer story, the names on the box, the personal touch at both ends of the supply chain — repeat purchases changed. Customers weren't just buying coffee. They were buying into something. They told other people about it. They took photos. They felt like they were part of what we were building.

That's not marketing. That's retention. That's referral. That's the compounding business value that positioning creates.

A strong positioning does something that advertising never fully can: it makes customers feel like choosing you was the right call. And people who feel that way become advocates, not just buyers.

## The Business Math Is Straightforward

Stronger positioning means:

- Higher prices customers accept because you're not a commodity
- Lower churn because they chose you specifically, not just something in your category
- Lower acquisition cost because advocates do your marketing
- Easier sales conversations because the right customer already wants what you are

None of that is a marketing outcome. All of it is a business outcome.

The small agency that repositioned as Agile Marketing Partners closed their next three deals faster than any deal in their history. Not because their service improved. Because their story stopped asking customers to compare them — and started asking customers to choose something new.

That's the business case for standing out.

## What Weak Positioning Actually Looks Like

You know you have a positioning problem when:

- Sales always ends up in a price conversation
- Customers leave for cheaper alternatives even when they liked you
- You struggle to explain in one sentence why someone should pick you over the obvious competitor
- Your marketing sounds like it could be copy-pasted onto a competitor's website without anyone noticing

That last one is the real test.

Take your homepage headline, your value proposition, your social bio. Replace your brand name with a competitor's. Does it still work? If yes, you're describing a category, not a position.

Positioning isn't about being different for the sake of being different. It's about being specific about what you uniquely are, so that the people who need that thing know they've found it.

---

## Starting is Simpler Than You Think

You don't need a brand agency or a positioning workshop or six months of strategy.

You need three things: what customers rave about, what you and only you can honestly claim, and who you're willing to walk away from.

The first is in your inbox right now. The second is in your own story — your background, your obsession, your constraints. The third is the uncomfortable conversation you keep avoiding.

Get those three things aligned and you're most of the way there.

The rest is just having the guts to say it out loud.
